### Abstract

A report may take minutes to produce while its reviewer still needs to check each claim. Lessons can be generated repeatedly, but students still need practice. Time saved at work may soon be filled by new assignments. Whether faster production improves life depends on how the released resources are used and who decides.

Beginning with the limits of time, attention and embodiment, this paper examines how expanding AI supply affects prices, work, relationships and distribution. Five volumes cover foundations, variables, derivations, models and research methods: 16 principles, 128 variables, and interactions among two to four variables. The same technology can reduce work or raise production targets, broaden educational opportunities or benefit well-resourced learners first. Demand, ownership, bargaining and institutions help explain these differences.

Each derivation states its assumptions, opposing outcomes and tests. Claims awaiting empirical study remain hypotheses. Its central question is whether making more things easier to do also gives people more discretionary time, more dependable relationships and greater scope to choose how they live.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; limited attention; scarcity; emotion regulation; task restructuring; social comparison; delegation; games; institutions; value pluralism.

### About me

I work at an artificial intelligence company in Hangzhou, China. These studies draw on my observations from product work, theoretical reading and practice. Beyond what technology can do next, I am interested in how it changes people’s time, work and relationships.

## 1. Research object: how technology enters everyday life

### 1.1 From capability to social relations

“What can AI do?” and “What does AI enable particular people to do?” are connected but distinct questions. The former concerns task performance; the latter also concerns costs, authority, skills, opportunities and organizational arrangements. Producing an excellent answer does not establish that a student understands it. Completing work does not establish that workers control the time saved. Continuous responses do not establish reciprocal relationships. These distinctions define the subject of this framework.

Units of analysis may include individuals, households, teams, platforms, industries or public institutions. They must not change silently during an argument. Twenty minutes saved by one person cannot directly establish higher industry employment, and higher company profits cannot directly establish greater social welfare. Cross-level inference requires mechanisms of aggregation, distribution and interaction. Systemic outcomes also cannot be reduced to individual character or effort.

Five questions organize inquiry: What task or relationship is changing? Who controls goals and action? Who bears costs and receives benefits? What feasible choices do affected people have? What would establish that the proposed mechanism caused the observed change? These questions admit beneficial, harmful and mixed effects within the same inspectable framework.

### 1.2 Three connected levels

The micro level concerns time, cognition, affect, desire, skill and self-understanding. The meso level concerns how households, occupations, organizations and platforms arrange tasks, visibility and responsibility. The macro level concerns markets, public institutions, infrastructure, culture and intergenerational opportunity. Effects can cross levels, but their transmission and temporal order must be specified.

A worker's higher output after adopting AI can coexist with shorter hours, proportionately greater workloads, or restructuring that reduces income. Distinguishing these outcomes requires evidence about demand, performance standards, ownership and bargaining, not simply additional capability measurements. Technical explanations delineate possibilities; social explanations examine why particular possibilities are realized.

### 1.3 Scope and source material

This framework synthesizes an existing AI sociology repository, course and book reading notes, summaries of technical and social reports, and thematic writing about content, work, relationships, education and judgment. Repeated ideas are consolidated into variables; narrative examples become research questions; forecasts and practitioner observations retain their evidential status. Notes do not substitute for original works. Unverified numbers, quotations and corporate cases are not presented as empirical findings here.

Public literature supplies adjacent foundations: limited attention, social comparison, signaling and asymmetric information, task displacement and new tasks, games and polycentric governance. This is a synthetic research program, not a claim that these topics were previously unstudied. An omission in one course cannot establish an omission across an entire discipline. [R01–R14]

## 2. A grammar of claims

### 2.1 Six distinct kinds of statement

| Code | Kind | Legitimate role | Invalid inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| D | Definition | Specify terms and objects | Treat a definition as empirical proof |
| C | Constraint | Identify a relevant boundary | Infer every concrete outcome |
| R | Empirical regularity | Summarize a supported relationship | Generalize automatically across populations and eras |
| H | Hypothesis | Propose a mechanism and prediction | Confuse persuasive narrative with validation |
| M | Conditional model proposition | Derive results from explicit assumptions | Assume those conditions already hold in reality |
| N | Normative judgment | State what merits protection or priority | Substitute values for causal explanation |

A 24-hour daily budget is a constraint within a specified timescale. “More content increases anxiety” is a conditional hypothesis. “Platforms should permit effective exit” includes a normative position. These statements may coexist without acquiring the same proof status. Each requires a different argument; placing them together does not give them equal evidential standing.

### 2.2 The limited meaning of “enduring”

The framework speaks of relatively stable constraints within a specified scope, rather than unrestricted claims about eternal human nature. Life, attention and bodies are finite, while their capacities, allocation and support conditions can change. Widespread relational needs do not establish identical needs in every person. Present experience cannot legislate unconditional outcomes for every future society.

Stable constraints delimit problems; variable parameters describe states within them; institutions allocate resources and authority; normative commitments determine which outcomes matter. Separating these layers makes the question of what persists analytically useful.

### 2.3 The role of formalization

Equations expose assumptions, identify variables and reveal omitted steps. Constructs that cannot be credibly quantified may first be examined qualitatively. Dignity, autonomy, money, relationships and health should not be added into an unexplained total score. Models must state units, functional forms and the origin of weights. Unestimated parameters serve only as thought-experiment inputs.

## 3. Sixteen foundational principles

### P01 · Time budgets and opportunity cost [C, D]

Within a defined horizon, time spent on one activity restricts time available for others. Delegation may reduce execution time while adding monitoring and repair. Savings require net accounting and an account of who controls their use. Without control, efficiency gains may become denser workloads rather than rest.

### P02 · Attention has capacity and allocation constraints [C, R]

Information can be stored, copied and filtered; effective human attention depends on workload, bodily state and switching costs. External memory and summarization may improve effective processing, so boundedness does not mean unchanging capacity. Studies should measure supply, selection and comprehension rather than dwell time alone. [R01]

### P03 · Embodiment and recovery belong in the model [C, H]

Work, care and experience occur in embodied lives. Sleep loss, fatigue and insufficient support may change AI use, but “emotional energy” is not an established fixed reservoir. Regulation demands, subjective fatigue, habituation and motivational change require separate measures. Replication problems in ego-depletion research caution against turning an attractive metaphor into a physiological law. [R02]

### P04 · Scarcity relates resources, needs and rules [D, M]

Finite resources need not be scarce in every setting, and expanded supply need not ensure access. Prices, queues, eligibility and location can produce practical exclusion. Scarcity must specify for whom, for what purpose, at what time, and under which rules. This separates natural limits, engineering bottlenecks and institutional exclusion.

### P05 · Indexed experience differs from reproducible records [D, H]

A conversation record can be copied, while the history of two parties jointly bearing consequences belongs to that relationship. This is an account of indexed experience, not a prediction that machine experience is impossible. Reproducible records do not establish complete relational substitutability; unique experience does not guarantee a price premium. Uniqueness, authenticity and price differ.

### P06 · Goals are plural; neither harmony nor conflict is assumed [D, H]

Users, workers, managers, platforms and public institutions may share some goals and differ on particular trade-offs. Greater capability does not logically align their goals. Divergence does not make cooperation impossible: contracts, reputation, participation and enforceable rules can change incentives. Identify the outcome dimensions on which interests differ.

### P07 · Separate capability, deployment, authority and outcomes [D, M]

Benchmark success is evidence of task capability. Deployment additionally requires data, maintenance, reliability, workflow integration and acceptance criteria. Acting requires appropriate authority. Outcomes also depend on others' responses and environmental change. “Can do” therefore does not imply “widely deployed” or “everyone benefits.”

### P08 · Separate marginal cost from total cost [D, M]

Cheaper text generation differs from cheaper trustworthy organizational decisions. Data preparation, integration, verification, failure, migration and governance can matter greatly. New cheap uses may increase total consumption. Cost comparisons require consistent quality and horizons, and should include costs shifted to workers, users or the environment.

### P09 · Value has multiple meanings [D, N]

Market price, use value, exchange scarcity, identity significance and moral standing are not interchangeable. Cheaper teaching may be more useful; less rare visual effects may remain beautiful; lower task wages do not reduce workers' dignity. “Devaluation” becomes researchable only after its meaning is specified.

### P10 · Signals depend on production and verification conditions [H, M]

When formerly costly surface features become cheap to imitate, their ability to distinguish underlying quality may weaken. Outcomes can include distrust or a shift toward auditable records, relationships and independent certification. Certification itself has costs and exclusion effects; demand for verification does not make every verifier trustworthy. [R03, R04]

### P11 · Preferences change; the sources of change matter [R, H]

Experience, relationships, culture and comparison shape preferences. Personalization can reveal needs or change the salience of options and aspirations. Assessing autonomy requires transparency, revocability and endorsement, rather than labeling all preference change manipulation. Social comparison also does not imply universal, permanent dissatisfaction. [R05, R06]

### P12 · Interactions may be nonlinear and recursive [M, H]

Two useful tools need not be more useful together. Faster generation with weaker verification may increase errors, whereas faster generation with verifiable processes may improve speed and quality. Thresholds, complementarity, substitution, delays and feedback alter additive expectations. After listing variables, an explanation must show how they interact.

### P13 · Distribution and rights change technological outcomes [H, N]

Demand, ownership and bargaining mediate the relationship between productivity and income. Greater total resources do not determine their distribution. Evaluating legitimacy requires explicit standards: efficiency, equality, need, contribution, freedom and participation can point in different directions. No single criterion silently replaces all others.

### P14 · Responsibility needs information, capacity and authority [H, N]

Adding a signatory need not create substantive oversight. Reviewers require relevant information, time, competence and authority to stop the process. Responsibility can be allocated through organizations, contracts, insurance and public institutions. It may support a valued role or impose a burden without control. “Someone is responsible” does not establish that risks have disappeared. [R07]

### P15 · Change follows multiple clocks and scales [H]

Model iteration, engineering deployment, learning and institutional revision have different cycles. Mismatches can generate transition costs but are not the sole origin of conflicting interests. Even synchronized systems can retain unequal ownership or divergent goals. Effects may also reverse between short and long horizons or local and aggregate levels.

### P16 · Revisability is a research commitment [N]

A framework must admit evidence against it. Explaining every observation through the same conclusion eliminates discriminatory power. Important hypotheses therefore require rival explanations, failure conditions and possible revisions. Openness means proportioning judgment to evidence, not refusing to judge.

## 4. Persistent constraints and changeable arrangements

### 4.1 Finite time is not a fixed destiny

No person can live every possible life, yet education, tools and cooperation can expand feasible choices. Finitude is therefore a starting point for studying choice quality, not a premise for pessimism. An hour saved on administration could support sleep, care, learning or more work; its value depends partly on who chooses its use.

Time constraints are uneven. Carers, shift workers and people with unstable incomes face different constraints from those with abundant support. Demanding identical rates of reskilling confuses resources with willpower. Research should distinguish total available time, uninterrupted time and control over time.

### 4.2 Attention scarcity does not make all content zero-sum

Within a fixed audience and period, attention to one item can displace another. But information may reduce search, improve decisions or introduce new subjects. Aggregate learning cannot be inferred directly from exposure competition on one platform. Attention allocation can contain zero-sum components while knowledge and cooperation create positive-sum benefits.

Simon's analysis of information-rich environments offers a starting point, not a universal traffic formula. Discovery, comprehension, memory, application and enjoyment differ. A pause may indicate surprise or lasting understanding. Treating duration as all value imports platform metrics into the theory without justification. [R01]

### 4.3 Emotional demand, recovery and adaptation

“Too much viewing leaves me feeling nothing” may describe fatigue, boredom, habituation, value mismatch or an absence of narrative meaning. Mechanisms and remedies differ. More stimulation need not resolve boredom; less stimulation need not restore interest. Lack of autonomy may make control central, while sleep loss may make stylistic novelty irrelevant.

Hedonic adaptation research directs attention to changing experience under repetition, but rates and degrees depend on circumstances. It does not establish that all happiness returns to zero. Adverse experiences can persist, and learning can deepen appreciation. Models should allow declining, increasing or stable responses and specify whether outcomes concern arousal, satisfaction or longer-term welfare. [R08]

### 4.4 Relative position and sufficient lives

A fixed ranking has only one first place. Recognition, however, has many dimensions, and desires need not consist entirely of rank. Basic needs, relationships, professional contribution and public participation can use different standards. Reducing desire to endless competitive superiority erases the possibility that institutions and culture change comparison pressures.

Changing reference groups may help individuals but cannot replace income protection, appropriate assessment or accessible public services. Where crucial opportunities remain rank-allocated, changing attitudes does not remove structural competition. Individual and institutional explanations should complement one another. [R05]

## 5. What may depreciate, and what may become scarcer

### 5.1 Four mechanisms of devaluation

Production-price decline means additional supply reduces unit prices at comparable quality and use. Declining rarity means a formerly exclusive form becomes more accessible. Signal degradation means an artifact no longer reliably indicates a capability. Relative-return decline means a useful skill earns less relative to alternatives.

These mechanisms can coincide or diverge. Cheaper high-quality translation may expand cross-language communication. Lower returns to some translation tasks do not make all language abilities unimportant. Task bundles, client needs and qualifications differ. Conclusions should identify tasks, quality, populations and horizons rather than assign a downward arrow to human intelligence as a whole.

### 5.2 Cognitive service prices and human judgment

Clear rules, assessable outputs and digitizable inputs provide useful starting conditions for automation analysis, but are not sufficient. Authorization, exception handling and error costs also matter. AI first changes how tasks are performed; jobs bundle tasks with organizational responsibilities. [R09]

Problem framing, acceptance and coordination may become more important at some stages without becoming permanent fortresses against automation. Ask when they are complementary, who can exercise them, how training is distributed, and whether demand supports their returns. Returns to these tasks can also change and require continued observation.

### 5.3 When good content becomes abundant

Quality is multidimensional: factual accuracy, narrative coherence, visual refinement, emotional relevance and practical usefulness differ. As polish becomes cheaper, audiences may rely more on relevance, track records and shared experience, or may continue to enjoy inexpensive sensory entertainment. Theory should not predetermine which preference is superior.

Expanded supply can also change discovery. Search, recommendation and social influence affect which works get evaluated. Cultural-market experiments show that social influence can alter concentration and unpredictability of success. This neither makes quality irrelevant nor directly establishes identical effects on every platform. [R10]

### 5.4 Escalating stimulation and exit from escalation

If exposure rewards immediate reactions and repeated forms lose their effect, producers may intensify stimulation. This path requires scarce exposure, optimizable response metrics and weak constraints from longer-term satisfaction. Escalation may stop or reverse if audiences avoid intensity or evaluation rewards comprehension and durable satisfaction.

“More stimulation becomes less effective” and “more stimulation wins exposure” can describe different stages, populations or outcomes. Clicks may rise while trust falls, and short-term viewing may increase while long-term retention declines. Specify outcomes and allow enough time to observe delayed effects.

### 5.5 Scarcity itself changes

Uninterrupted time, credible verification, practical care, effective exit, decision rights and stable relationships may become harder to obtain than generated content in particular settings. Greater scarcity does not ensure higher prices or fair compensation. Care is a reminder that high social need need not produce sufficient market payment.

Rather than a permanent list of appreciating assets, the framework proposes a procedure: identify which constraint technology relaxes, locate new bottlenecks and their controllers, then examine substitution, sharing or institutional relief. New scarcity may create opportunities, rents or exclusion; its normative sign is not predetermined.

## 6. Seven mechanisms and their branches

### 6.1 Generation expansion

Lower marginal costs ease potential supply expansion, but actual expansion also requires demand, channels, computing and organization. Greater supply may improve access or add repetition and noise. Results depend on matching, verification and whether unmet needs are served.

A beneficial path combines cheap supply and good retrieval to serve minority languages or niche tasks. Another combines volume and weak verification, shifting evaluation burdens to users. Both may occur on one platform. Compare beneficiaries and those bearing costs, not publication counts alone.

### 6.2 Delegation chains

Delegation can involve retrieval, advice, selection, execution and continuing authority. Each level requires goals, information boundaries, action scope and revocation terms. Advice permits a subsequent human decision; continuing action requires authorization across future contexts. “AI use” is too broad a treatment label for all these arrangements.

Delegation can release cognitive capacity while increasing dependence. Portable data, clear records and substitute suppliers may make dependence manageable. Concentrated histories, workflows and relationship access may make exit progressively costly. The transition from convenience to dependence requires longitudinal observation.

### 6.3 Price anchors and task restructuring

Technology may change buyers' expectations of reasonable prices and delivery speed. Buyers may demand lower quotes, more output or broader services. Workers' outcomes depend on access to complementary tasks and whether new demand offsets substitution. Displacement, new tasks and demand expansion belong in the same analysis. [R09]

Some people may lose in the short run while an industry expands later. Expansion need not compensate the same people. “New jobs will eventually appear” omits transition timing, geography and skill matching. Distributional analysis follows populations rather than substituting aggregate headcounts for individual histories.

### 6.4 Signals and verification infrastructure

When polished text, voices and images become easier to generate, their evidential role for identity, competence and fact may change. Failure of old signals need not produce universal skepticism: reliable sources, independent tests and auditable processes can supply new grounds. Verifiers nevertheless have incentives and limitations of their own. [R03, R04]

Automated verification creates a second-order question: under what conditions is the verifier trustworthy? More labels alone cannot resolve it. Independence, sampling, reproducibility, error disclosure and correction matter. Warranted trust need not require every individual to repeat all specialist work.

### 6.5 Skill formation and offloading

Tools can promote learning through timely feedback and more practice, or reduce independent skill by replacing practice. Assisted completion and unaided learning are distinct outcomes. Both may matter, but differently for education and adaptation.

Expert intuition requires learnable environments and meaningful feedback; years of experience are insufficient evidence. Fluent but incorrect automated feedback may stabilize error. Verifiable feedback and retained active attempts may accelerate learning. Measure immediate performance, delayed retention and transfer separately. [R11]

### 6.6 Relational complementarity and substitution

Accessible, persistent responses may support expression, practice and information, or alter expectations of human interaction. Outcomes depend on existing support, patterns of use, commercial objectives and the kind of relational friction involved. Added responses may help people lacking support, while displacement of available relationships may follow a different mechanism.

Higher loneliness among users does not establish that use caused loneliness; lonely people may be more likely to adopt. Interaction frequency, feeling understood, practical help, reciprocity and data exposure should be distinguished. The framework retains opposite paths rather than predefining a technology as either an enemy or a complete substitute for relationships.

### 6.7 Institutions and collective action

When individually advantageous choices jointly increase stimulation, verification or work burdens, collective action and rule coordination become relevant. Nash equilibrium means no participant benefits from unilateral deviation under specified strategies and payoffs. It does not mean social optimality or guaranteed convergence. [R12]

Rules can alter incentives, but enforcement costs, representation and exclusion also matter. Polycentric governance invites inquiry into arrangements at multiple scales, without presuming a single center or wholly spontaneous order works everywhere. [R13]

## 7. From two variables to four

### 7.1 Two variables: a minimal mechanism

Connecting two concepts is not a derivation. Specify input changes, the outcome and held conditions. More content plus limited attention may reduce average attention per item only under a fixed audience, horizon and selection process. If content replaces ineffective search, understanding can improve; if the audience expands, attention per item need not fall.

Each two-variable card supplies contrasting branches and observable differences. Its purpose is a minimal researchable comparison, not a two-word explanation of society. A mechanism may fail because the input did not change or because another condition interrupted transmission.

### 7.2 Three variables: identify a reversal condition

A third variable often moderates a relationship. Falling generation costs and higher task substitutability may reduce some labor demand. Task-demand elasticity can introduce expansion that offsets part of the reduction. This is a change in the strength or direction of a relationship, not the addition of another label.

Moderation differs from mediation. Verification costs may arise downstream of supply expansion, or an existing verification regime may condition expansion's effects. Roles depend on time and the research question, not on variable names alone. [R14]

### 7.3 Four variables: connect technology, behavior and institutions

Task capability, verification capacity, oversight load and responsibility allocation can jointly shape human review. Responsibility without competence or time may produce nominal sign-off. Capacity, manageable load and stopping authority may support substantive review. These conditions still require outcome measurement; listing four safeguards does not prove safety.

There are 8,128 unordered pairs, 341,376 triples and 10,668,000 quadruples among 128 variables. These 11,017,504 tuples are candidates, not established causal laws. The project supplies an enumerator and develops 64 mechanism cards. Directions, temporal orders and states generate further models, so the framework does not claim to exhaust reality.

### 7.4 Eight operators

| Operator | Question | Required care |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | Do burdens accumulate? | Comparable scales; no double counting |
| Complementarity | Does one input raise another's effect? | Joint versus separate use |
| Substitution | Does one reduce the need for another? | Fixed task and quality |
| Bottleneck | Does the weakest stage limit the system? | Necessary stage dependencies |
| Threshold | Does the mechanism change beyond a level? | Observations on both sides |
| Feedback | Does the outcome alter the input? | Temporal order and delay |
| Moderation | Does a third variable change a relationship? | Interaction versus grouped correlation |
| Distribution | Who receives the aggregate result? | Groups, rights and cost bearers |

These are ways to construct questions, not new natural laws. Combining operators increases the assumptions requiring examination.

## 8. What game theory contributes

### 8.1 Specify players before equilibrium

A game needs participants, strategies, information, payoffs and timing. Producers, platforms and audiences need not share goals, and audiences are heterogeneous. Payoffs involving income, learning, satisfaction and reputation require a perspective and horizon. Without them, “Nash equilibrium” often merely renames a phenomenon.

Volume IV provides models of stimulation competition, coordination, auditing and bargaining. Numbers illustrate logic, not platform evidence or estimated behavioral parameters. Different parameters can change equilibria or create multiple equilibria. Research asks which payoff structures institutions actually sustain and how interventions might change them.

### 8.2 Equilibrium is not satisfaction

In a prisoner's-dilemma representation of stimulation, both parties may prefer mutual restraint while individually preferring escalation. If intensity reduces returns or long-term reputation matters, that ordering may fail. Verify the game structure before choosing the equilibrium concept.

Low adoption caused by coordination and low welfare caused by conflicting interests require different explanations. Standards and shared expectations may address the former; changing payoffs or rights may be needed for the latter. Better technical understanding alone does not resolve distributional conflict.

### 8.3 Bounded rationality and change

Real goals may be unclear, strategies evolve, and learning and imitation affect conduct. Static equilibrium is a starting point. Repeated interaction, reputation, networks and institutional learning may be useful extensions, but more complex models are not automatically more realistic. Match complexity to the question and available evidence.

## 9. Goals, uncertainty and anxiety

### 9.1 Decompose unanswerable questions

“Will AI replace me?” combines tasks, income, identity and opportunity. Disaggregation asks which tasks are substitutable, which require complementary inputs, how compensation changes, which transition resources are accessible, and which costs need collective provision. This creates grounds for action without promising the elimination of risk.

“Am I falling behind?” requires a reference group and an actual goal. Following every tool may not advance a person's priorities. Knowledge choices depend on tasks, available time and verifiable gains. But resource deprivation must not be relabeled unclear goals: personal purposes and public conditions both matter.

### 9.2 Three kinds of certainty

Certainty about technical forecasts, institutional commitments and personal values differs. Someone can be unsure when a model will diffuse yet know which relationships and conditions matter. Another may understand performance while lacking stable income or exit options. Technical knowledge cannot replace social protection, though it can clarify evidential grounds.

The framework's normative orientation is to expand feasible choices people have reason to endorse, protecting explanation, contestation, refusal and participation. This is an explicit value position, not an inevitable deduction from technical parameters. Readers may share the tools while disagreeing about distributional principles.

### 9.3 Conditions for reducing anxiety

Decomposable uncertainty, identifiable responsibility and feasible action may help more than repeated declarations of optimism. This remains a research hypothesis, not a therapeutic promise. Some anxiety reflects real circumstances; analysis should clarify those circumstances rather than use reassurance to normalize unreasonable arrangements.

Communication should distinguish what is known, unknown and observable. Literary images can illuminate time and choice without replacing evidence. Memorable sentences can condense questions without deleting their conditions. The easier an idea is to repeat, the more important it is that its boundaries survive repetition.

## 10. An open research agenda

First, where does scarcity move after generation becomes cheaper: retrieval, verification, relationships or access? Do different groups face different bottlenecks? Can several constraints improve without equally severe new bottlenecks? That final possibility is a genuine counterexample the framework must admit.

Second, how are productivity gains divided among wages, profits, prices, taxation and discretionary time? Which institutions change that allocation, and at what cost? Evaluate protective effects and exclusion, not merely the existence of written rules.

Third, which tools increase active practice, and which improve only immediate completion? How do persistent responses affect support networks across populations? Follow longer-term outcomes and retain participant accounts rather than explaining life changes through a single satisfaction score.

Fourth, do more content and life scripts expand feasible choices? How is shared factual ground maintained? How do groups negotiate values that cannot be fully exchanged? Experiments, surveys, interviews, historical comparison and institutional analysis may all contribute.

Finally, which variables should be merged, which are missing, which mechanisms duplicate one another, and which derivations will fail against new evidence? Preserving these questions allows the framework to remain useful as technology and society change together.

## 11. Reading and use

Volume I provides concepts and principles. Volume II defines variables and candidate measures. Volume III develops 64 conditional mechanism cards. Volume IV offers inspectable formal models. Volume V specifies evidence, identification, revision and citation practices. V, P, D, M and R identifiers locate variables, principles, derivations, models and references; they do not rank truth.

Read every path alongside its opposing branch. Public forecasts additionally require a horizon, observable outcome, baseline or defensible probability, and revision triggers. Thought experiments should retain that label. Retaining assumptions and revision records lets readers judge where an explanation holds and where it fails.
